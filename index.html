pitch an idea

/pɪtʃ ən aɪˈdɪə/

step on someone’s toes

/step ɒn ˈsʌmwʌnz təʊz/

idiom

Upset someone by interfering with work or decisions they consider theirs.

step into someone’s shoes

/step ˈɪntə ˈsʌmwʌnz ʃuːz/

idiom

Take over another person’s job or responsibilities.

step into + possessive + shoes

go to waste

/ɡəʊ tə weɪst/

phrase

Remain unused when something could have been useful.

Let’s share the research so that our work doesn’t go to waste.

a relocation bonus

/ə ˌriːləʊˈkeɪʃən ˈbəʊnəs/

noun phrase

An extra payment from an employer to help with moving for a job.

Ask whether the relocation bonus will cover the deposit.

receive / offer a relocation bonus

an acquisition

/ən ˌækwɪˈzɪʃən/

noun

The purchase of a company, or a company that has been bought.

The acquisition gives the business access to a new market.

an acquisition / acquire a company

Toes: a boundary

Uninvited interference can upset someone. This idiom signals concern about that boundary.

Shoes: a handover

Taking someone’s place can be welcome. It does not automatically imply conflict.

Same word, different meaning

Step into her shoes can mean take over her role. Put yourself in her shoes means imagine her situation.

Section 3 of 10 · New vocabulary

What makes an offer worth taking?

Three expressions for weighing up an opportunity.

a trade-off

/ə ˈtreɪd ɒf/

noun

A choice in which gaining one benefit means accepting a disadvantage.

The trade-off is less security in exchange for more freedom.

a trade-off between A and B

room for manoeuvre

/ruːm fə məˈnuːvə/

noun phrase

Freedom to adjust a plan or decision.

The budget is fixed, but we have room for manoeuvre on the deadline.

leave / allow room for manoeuvre

be thrown in at the deep end

/bi θrəʊn ɪn ət ðə ˈdiːp end/

idiom

Be given a difficult task with little preparation or help.

On my first day, I was thrown in at the deep end and asked to lead a meeting.

be thrown in at the deep end

A role with a catch

Written for this lesson

“The new role pays more, but I’d lose the flexibility I have now. That’s the main trade-off. Before accepting, I’ll ask whether there’s any room for manoeuvre on working hours. I’d also need a proper handover. I don’t mind a challenge, but I don’t want to be thrown in at the deep end with no one to ask for help.”

Would you accept? What would you ask for first?

Section 6 of 10 · Grammar

Make your conditions clear

Revision · although, despite, nevertheless

Although + clause: Although the role is demanding, I’m interested.

Despite + noun / -ing: Despite the workload, I’m interested. Despite feeling nervous, I applied.

Nevertheless: The role is demanding. Nevertheless, I’m interested.

Use a full stop or semicolon before nevertheless. Avoid “despite of”.

New · C1 grammar

Inversion for emphasis

In a formal proposal, move a limiting or negative expression to the beginning to emphasise a condition or benefit. Put the auxiliary or modal before the subject.

Only if + condition + auxiliary/modal + subject + verb

Normal: I will accept only if there is a proper handover.
Emphatic: Only if there is a proper handover will I accept.

Keep normal word order inside the if-clause: “if there is”, not “if is there”. Invert the main clause: “will I accept”.

Not only + auxiliary/modal + subject + verb, but… also…

Normal: The role would give me experience and more responsibility.
Emphatic: Not only would the role give me experience, but it would also give me more responsibility.

Without an existing auxiliary, use do / does / did and the base verb: “Not only does the role offer training, but it also includes mentoring.” The second clause keeps normal word order.

When is inversion useful?

Use it for a deliberate emphasis in a pitch or formal email. One clear example is enough. In ordinary conversation, normal word order often sounds more relaxed.

No inversion when the expression stays later: “I will accept only if…” / “The role not only offers training but also…”

A conditional yes

Written for this lesson

“There’s a real trade-off between the salary and my free time. Only if there is room for manoeuvre on working hours will I accept. I’d also want a mentor so I’m not thrown in at the deep end. With that support, the offer is attractive: not only would I gain experience, but I would also build a useful network.”

Find the condition and the two benefits. Which words have moved before their subjects?

Check the pattern

Condition: flexible hours. Benefits: experience and contacts. Will comes before I; would comes before I. The if-clause keeps normal order.
